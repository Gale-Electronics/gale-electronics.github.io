# GT2101 – Nigel Hobden Oral History and Emerging Development Hypothesis

Note prepared: 7 October 2026<br>
Primary source: Conversation with Nigel Hobden, 29 September 2026<br>
Status: Oral history and research hypothesis. Not all information below has been independently verified.

This is a later write-up of additional details from the same conversation described in the [historical research record](/GT2101/research-notes/#nigel-hobdens-recollection-september-2026), not a separate interview.

---

## 1. Why did Ira Gale want the GT2101?

A significant part of the GT2101 story may have been missed by concentrating on the precision of its speed control.

Nigel Hobden recalled that Ira Gale was particularly frustrated by subtle pitch errors in older vinyl records. According to Nigel, some records could be slightly wrong in pitch even though they were intended to play at the standard speed. The error could be small enough that most listeners would not notice it, but Ira was particularly sensitive to it.

Nigel's recollection is that Ira wanted a turntable which allowed the listener to make an extremely fine adjustment to playback speed, and therefore pitch.

The nominal speed, such as 33⅓ rpm, was therefore not necessarily the final objective. It was the reference point.

A record could be started at its nominal speed and the control tower could display the operating speed. If the listener considered the pitch of the recording to be slightly incorrect, the speed could then be adjusted manually by a very small amount.

The important design requirement may therefore have been:

> **Not simply to make the turntable run accurately at 33⅓ rpm, but to allow the listener to deliberately and precisely alter playback speed around that reference point.**

This provides a possible explanation for the combination of:

- precision servo control;
- optical speed feedback;
- fine manual speed adjustment;
- the Helipot control;
- digital speed indication;
- and the separate control tower.

The technical operation should still be checked against the surviving schematics before this is presented as established design intent.

## 2. A possible change in how the GT2101 should be understood

Earlier accounts can give the impression that Ira Gale was primarily a commercial intermediary who obtained a turntable and then engaged DCA to develop the technology around it.

The new evidence suggests that this interpretation may be too simplistic.

A more useful working hypothesis is that Ira Gale had a particular performance requirement and wanted to create a turntable capable of doing something existing turntables did not do.

The emerging picture is therefore potentially:

Ira Gale identifies a problem with pitch accuracy → defines a requirement for extremely fine variable-speed control → sources or commissions a suitable motor/feedback system → develops the control concept → brings in engineering assistance to realise the electronics, servo system and mechanical implementation.

This does not mean that Ira Gale personally designed every part of the GT2101.

DCA may have carried out substantial engineering work, particularly in the electronics, servo control, PCB design, mechanical engineering and production implementation.

The distinction is important:

> **The person who originated the requirement is not necessarily the person who engineered every component needed to fulfil it.**

The precise division of responsibility between Ira Gale, DCA and the various component suppliers remains an open research question.

## 3. Motor and optical encoder

Nigel recalled that the GT2101 motor was associated with Litton, but also recalled the name Inland in connection with the motor.

Nigel further recalled occasions when the motor had to be opened and the optical disc adjusted with considerable precision. He emphasised that this was a delicate operation and that mistakes were not desirable.

This is potentially significant because it suggests that the motor and optical feedback system were not necessarily treated as a completely untouchable, fixed assembly.

The current archive contains more than one account of the motor:

- one recollection describes the motors as finished Litton units;
- another technical description identifies an Inland brushless DC motor with a Litton 600-line optical encoder.

These statements should currently be treated as unresolved rather than choosing one as correct.

Possible explanations include:

1. Litton supplied complete motor/encoder assemblies.
2. Inland supplied some of the motors.
3. Inland supplied the motor while Litton supplied the optical encoder.
4. Different suppliers were used at different stages of GT2101 development or production.
5. The surviving accounts refer to different versions of the motor assembly.
6. The names were subsequently conflated in recollections or secondary sources.

No production changeover should currently be asserted.

---

## 4. Ira Gale and the motor suppliers

Nigel also recalled that Ira Gale had fallen out with one of the motor manufacturers.

This could be significant if it explains why both Litton and Inland appear in surviving accounts.

Possible explanations include a supplier disagreement, availability problems, production limitations, financial issues, dissatisfaction with the supplied motor, or a deliberate decision to move to another manufacturer.

However, none of these possibilities is currently established.

It is therefore important not to turn the recollection into:

> "Ira fell out with Litton and moved to Inland."

That remains only one possible explanation.

The research question should instead be:

> **Were different motor suppliers used during GT2101 development or production, and if so, why?**

The surviving motors, labels, wiring, contemporary documentation and supplier catalogues may eventually answer this.

---

## 5. Litton, Inland and the possibility of Sao Win

The relationship between Ira Gale, the motor supplier(s), the optical encoder and Sao Win also requires reassessment.

There is currently no independent evidence establishing that Sao Win designed the GT2101 motor.

However, it remains possible that Sao Win had a role in the electronics or control system, particularly if he had relevant knowledge of precision servo control and optical feedback.

Nigel's recollections about the importance of the fine pitch adjustment raise a further possibility: that people involved with Gale were deliberately seeking a technical solution to Ira Gale's requirement for extremely fine and controllable speed adjustment.

It is therefore reasonable to retain the possibility that Sao Win contributed to the electronics or control concept, but this must remain explicitly marked as a hypothesis.

At present there is insufficient evidence to state that Sao Win designed the motor, designed the control tower, or worked directly with Ira Gale on the GT2101.

Further evidence is required.

---

## 6. DCA's role

The emerging evidence does not mean that DCA had no involvement in the GT2101.

Rather, it suggests that the question should be framed differently.

DCA may have been responsible for turning an existing requirement into a working engineering system.

Potential DCA responsibilities include:

- control electronics;
- servo circuitry;
- PCB design and assembly;
- interface electronics between the motor and encoder;
- mechanical engineering;
- production engineering;
- testing and servicing.

Nigel's recollection that the motor itself sometimes had to be opened and its optical disc adjusted may indicate that DCA personnel were involved in the practical integration and servicing of the motor/encoder system.

The exact division of responsibility remains to be established.

The archive should therefore avoid both extremes:

> **"DCA designed the entire GT2101."**

and:

> **"DCA had nothing to do with the motor or turntable."**

The evidence may ultimately show a more complicated collaboration in which Ira Gale defined the requirement and drove the project, while DCA engineered substantial parts of the implementation.

---

## 7. PCB variations

Surviving GT2101 control towers contain PCB variations.

Because relatively few GT2101 turntables appear to have been produced, differences between surviving boards may preserve stages of development or production which would normally be difficult to identify in a much larger production run.

Possible explanations include:

- development boards;
- production revisions;
- changes to the motor or encoder interface;
- component availability;
- externally manufactured boards populated by DCA;
- boards manufactured directly by DCA;
- replacement boards;
- or individual engineering modifications.

The manufacturer of the PCBs has not yet been established.

This should be investigated directly with Nigel Hobden.

The archive should not automatically describe every physical difference as a formal PCB revision until its purpose and chronology have been established.

---

## 8. Gore-Tex cables

Nigel also recalled Gore-Tex in connection with the unusual coiled cables used with the GT2101.

The recollection includes:

- the coiled mains power cable running from the control tower to the wall socket;
- the coiled cable running from the control tower to the motor.

The unusual cable construction appears to have been selected for its ability to provide a flexible coiled connection.

The exact relationship between Gore-Tex and these cables remains to be independently documented.

At present this should therefore be recorded as:

> **Nigel Hobden's recollection of Gore-Tex as the cable supplier.**

It should not yet be stated that Gore-Tex manufactured the complete cable assemblies specifically for Gale, or that the cables were handmade by Gore-Tex, until documentary or further oral evidence confirms this.

## 9. An emerging development model

The evidence currently suggests a possible development sequence which is more complicated than the older simplified accounts.

One possible model is:

Ira Gale identifies a problem with pitch accuracy in vinyl

↓

Ira wants exceptionally fine manual control of playback speed

↓

A precision motor and optical feedback system is sourced or developed

↓

Litton and/or Inland become involved in the motor/encoder system

↓

The motor/encoder arrangement is integrated with Gale's turntable concept

↓

DCA develops substantial electronics and engineering required to control it

↓

The control tower provides the user interface, fine speed reference and display

↓

The servo system continuously adjusts the motor to follow the selected speed

This is a working hypothesis, not an established chronology.

It provides a framework for future evidence without assigning responsibility prematurely.

---

## 10. Important caution concerning oral history

Nigel Hobden's recollections are valuable because he was directly involved with DCA and the GT2101 project.

However, it cannot currently be assumed that every detail remembered today derives solely from his direct experience.

In particular, the names Litton and Inland already occur in earlier GaleAudio-derived material. Nigel may therefore be recalling information from his direct experience, information encountered later, or a combination of the two.

For this reason, the archive should preserve:

- what Nigel says he remembers;
- when he said it;
- what can be confirmed from surviving hardware;
- what appears in contemporary documents;
- and what remains a hypothesis.

This distinction is particularly important because the archive is intended to separate primary evidence from later claims and inherited secondary information.

## 11. Research priorities

The new evidence creates several important research questions:

1. Who supplied the original GT2101 motor?
2. What exactly did Litton supply?
3. What exactly did Inland Motor supply?
4. Were Litton and Inland both used during GT2101 production?
5. Did the supplier change during production?
6. Did Ira Gale have a dispute with a motor manufacturer?
7. Was the optical encoder supplied separately from the motor?
8. What was the exact Litton encoder model and disc specification?
9. Why did some motors require the optical disc to be physically adjusted?
10. Who manufactured the GT2101 PCBs?
11. Were the PCBs made by DCA or externally fabricated?
12. Do PCB variations correspond to different motor/encoder configurations?
13. What exactly was Sao Win's involvement, if any?
14. Did Sao Win contribute to the electronics or servo-control concept?
15. Who supplied the Gore-Tex cables?
16. Were the cables specially manufactured for Gale/DCA?
17. Can surviving motors, wiring labels and connectors identify the original supplier?
18. Can contemporary supplier catalogues identify a motor matching the GT2101 hardware?

## 12. Current conclusion

The GT2101 should not currently be described simply as a turntable to which sophisticated electronics were subsequently added.

The emerging evidence suggests that Ira Gale may have had a much more active role in defining what the turntable was intended to achieve.

His concern appears to have extended beyond simply achieving an accurate 33⅓ rpm. The more interesting requirement may have been the ability to make extremely fine, deliberate changes to playback speed in order to correct the perceived pitch of individual recordings.

This could explain why the GT2101 required such an elaborate servo, encoder and control-tower system.

It also raises the possibility that Ira Gale was directly involved in selecting, commissioning or changing suppliers for the motor and feedback system.

DCA's role may consequently be better understood as a major engineering and implementation role within a project driven by a requirement originating with Ira Gale, rather than necessarily being the originator of the entire concept.

The possible involvement of Sao Win remains unresolved. It is reasonable to investigate whether he contributed to the electronics or servo-control solution, but there is currently insufficient evidence to state this as fact.

The Litton/Inland question also remains unresolved.

The archive should therefore treat the current model as a developing hypothesis and continue to prioritise physical evidence, contemporary documents and independently sourced testimony over later secondary accounts.
